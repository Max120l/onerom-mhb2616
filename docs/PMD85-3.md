# The PMD 85-3 side: memory map, socket wiring, and what remains to establish

## Memory map

From MAME's `src/mame/tesla/pmd85.cpp`, which encodes the -3 precisely:

**Startup map** (in force from reset until the CPU's first I/O write):

| range | read | write |
|-------|------|-------|
| 0000–1FFF | ROM mirror | RAM |
| 2000–BFFF | ROM mirrors | RAM |
| C000–DFFF | ROM mirror | Video RAM #1 |
| E000–FFFF | ROM | Video RAM #2 |

The 8080 comes out of reset at 0x0000 and finds the monitor's first byte
there because the whole 64 KB reads as mirrored ROM. Reads and writes go to
*different* memories — a write-through arrangement, cleared as a whole by
the first OUT instruction.

**Normal map** (after the first I/O write):

| range | contents |
|-------|----------|
| 0000–BFFF | RAM |
| C000–DFFF | Video RAM #1 |
| E000–FFFF | ROM read / Video RAM #2 write |

Consequences for a board in a ROM socket:

- **The mirrors are the machine's doing, not the chips'.** The sockets see
  A0–A10 and their selects; whether the CPU address was 0x0005 or 0xE005 is
  the decoder's business. The board serves whatever it is strobed for and
  the mirror behaviour comes out right by construction.
- **The ROM shares E000–FFFF with VRAM #2 writes**, so selects may arrive
  denser than CPU instruction timing suggests. Irrelevant to correctness
  (the loop serves every cycle it is selected for), worth knowing when
  reading a scope trace.

## The socket wiring — measured from the schematic

DOSKA CPU, 1 PK 280 77. Sockets DS4 (E), DS5 (D), DS6 (C), DS7 (B) — the
schematic prints DS6's letter as "0", but the real machine says C; the
sheet carries a few such typos, which is itself worth knowing — all
wired identically for address and data (JEDEC 2716 positions), with:

| signal | pin | wiring |
|--------|-----|--------|
| /CS | 20 | **pair select, active low**: DS4+DS5 share one net, DS6+DS7 the other |
| PR | 18 | **A11**: straight into DS4 and DS6, inverted into DS5 and DS7 |
| pin 21 | 21 | +5 V rail, with pin 24 |

This answers what used to be this file's question 1, and better than any of
the guessed arrangements: the socket hands the board a genuine address bit
(A11 on PR) *and* a half-decoded select (/CS covering a 4 KB pair). From
one socket the board therefore knows everything except which pair an access
to the *other* pair belongs to — and that is exactly one flying lead away.

What the wiring means for each firmware mode:

| mode | wires | gate | bank bits |
|------|-------|------|-----------|
| STATIC | 0 | /CS low AND PR at this chip's level | fixed |
| PAIR | 0 | /CS low | A11 = PR (± invert) |
| FULL8K | 1 | /CS low OR X1 low | A12 = which select; A11 = PR |
| HOTSPOT | 0 | /CS low AND PR at this chip's level | magic reads |

The FULL8K lead runs from the *other* pair's pin 20 (either empty socket of
that pair) to the board's X1 pad. X1 is pulled up on the board, so a
detached lead reads "other pair not selected" and the board degrades to
serving its own pair rather than serving wrong data.

## Still to establish

### 1. Which pair and which PR sense is which bank — SETTLED

The schematic fixed the *structure* (pairs, A11 polarity per socket); the
Infoserver's ROM archive fixed the *assignment*. Its per-chip `.bin`
files match the combined `monit3B.rom` byte-for-byte at these offsets —
not read off a table, but verified by comparing every byte:

| socket | letter | bank | monitor offset | CPU address | `MHB_SOCKET_PAIR` | `MHB_PR_INVERT` |
|--------|--------|------|----------------|-------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| DS4 | E | 0 | 0x0000–0x07FF | E000–E7FF | 0 | OFF |
| DS5 | D | 1 | 0x0800–0x0FFF | E800–EFFF | 0 | ON |
| DS6 | C | 2 | 0x1000–0x17FF | F000–F7FF | 1 | OFF |
| DS7 | B | 3 | 0x1800–0x1FFF | F800–FFFF | 1 | ON |

(`MHB_SOCKET_BANK` for STATIC/HOTSPOT is the bank column.) The letters
descend E→B as addresses ascend, matching the archive's own note that the
chips sit "in order E-D-C-B from the microprocessor" — DS4 nearest the
CPU. Two corollaries fall out:

- **The chips are selected when PR reads low.** DS4 holds bank 0
  (A11 = 0) and its PR is straight A11, so its select level is low — and
  the inverted sockets agree. This is also why MHB 2716 substitutions
  historically worked: the 2716's /CE lands on PR and is active low too.
- **The board's `MHB_PR_INVERT` is a pure socket property** (ON exactly
  for DS5/DS7), since the firmware's bank bit is A11 itself, not the
  chip's select sense.

If a boot ever contradicts this table, re-check the build switches before
doubting it: pairs swapped → wrong half executes, ROM test fails
immediately → `MHB_SOCKET_PAIR`. Banks within a pair swapped →
`MHB_PR_INVERT`. Do not reorder the image to compensate.

**Which reference image.** Monitor versions are not interchangeable;
the -3B machine wants `monit3B.rom`. Reference points (whole-file and
per-bank CRC32, banks 0..3), computed from the Infoserver archive
(`PMD85-rom-files`, RM-TEAM 2017):

| image | whole-file | bank 0 | bank 1 | bank 2 | bank 3 |
|-------|-----------|--------|--------|--------|--------|
| `monit3.rom` (plain -3; byte-identical to MAME's `pmd85-3.bin`) | `83E22C47` | `C443BE75` | `4EE6CE97` | `0C1E3552` | `83E9522F` |
| `monit3B.rom` (-3B) | `878F08DF` | `0A41D057` | `4EE6CE97` | `0C1E3552` | `9A7BBA86` |

The two versions differ **only in banks 0 and 3** (chips E and B): per
the archive's notes, 3B's ROLL/ROLL2/WRCHAR/ERASE routines avoid the SP
register, plus the compatibility-mode routine at FB64h. Service
consequence: C and D chips interchange freely between a -3 and a -3B;
E and B do not.

For machines with readable chips, the dump-based route remains in the
tools: `rom_checksum.py --identify ref.rom dump.bin` names a chip's bank
even from a degraded dump (mismatch counts against all four banks are
printed, so a marginal verdict looks marginal), and one identified chip
fixes all four sockets by XOR: pair-mate `b^1`, other pair `b^2`/`b^3`.
Do not substitute PMD 85-1 dumps for any of this — the -1 monitor is a
different 4 KB program and resembles no -3 bank.

**All four chips bad?** The cleanest case, not the worst: the board
serves everything (FULL8K, or two PAIR boards), the table above supplies
the mapping, and the machine's own startup ROM test is the confirmation.
Nothing left on the bus to fight, nothing to version-match against.

**Mixing sources when some chips are bad:** decide per bank where its
bytes come from. Serving the reference image for *all* banks (PAIR or
FULL8K) is the safe default — the monitor is one program, and its banks
cross-call, so a version mismatch between a surviving original chip and a
reference-served neighbour is a real (if unlikely) hazard. Before leaving
a good original in its socket next to reference-served banks, `--compare`
its dump against the reference: byte-identical means same version, mix
freely; differences mean find the matching version on the Infoserver or
serve all four banks from the board.

### 2. Is /CS qualified by the read strobe?

The original chips drive their outputs whenever /CS+PR select them, so
either /CS is qualified by /MEMR (asserts only on reads) or the machine
tolerates ROM outputs during write cycles into the mirrored region by
never letting decoded ROM selects and write strobes coincide. The original
chips' survival proves the arrangement is safe for *chip-shaped* behaviour
— and the firmware gates exactly as the chips do, so it inherits that
safety. Nothing to change; worth one scope glance at pin 20 against /WR
during the startup-map phase if anything ever looks off.

### 3. Serving beyond the monitor

The -3 also takes ROM cartridges through the ROM module interface (8255,
not in the CPU address space) — out of scope here; a cartridge is its own
project. HOTSPOT mode plus custom software is this project's answer for
"more than 8 KB", per docs/ROADMAP.md.

## The bring-up ladder

Each rung is cheap and rules out a class of fault before the next spends
time on it. Instrument first, monitor image last.

1. **Bench, no machine.** Selftest image, STATIC mode with
   `-DMHB_STATIC_IGNORE_PR=ON` and `-DMHB_SOCKET_BANK=<n>`, read through a
   2716 programmer (which parks PR at one level — that is what IGNORE_PR is
   for). `check_selftest.py` must come back clean for all four bank
   settings. Never take an IGNORE_PR build near a machine.
2. **Machine, STATIC, selftest.** Board in one socket, *that socket's
   original removed*, machine on. The monitor fails its ROM test and stops
   — expected; the board's LED must show serving (steady flicker). This
   proves selects, levels and timing in-circuit without trusting contents.
3. **Machine, STATIC, real bank.** The socket's own bank of the monitor
   image, `MHB_SOCKET_BANK`/`MHB_PR_INVERT` per the checksum table from
   question 1. The machine boots exactly as stock: the first genuinely
   useful configuration — a 2616 replacement.
4. **Machine, PAIR.** Pair-mate removed too, `MHB_SOCKET_PAIR` set, same
   image. Boot as stock proves the PR-as-A11 serving live. Two chips
   replaced, zero wires.
5. **Machine, FULL8K.** All four originals out, one lead from the other
   pair's pin 20 to X1. Boot as stock, ROM test pass on the -3's own
   startup screen — the machine checks its own checksum, which is a better
   instrument than anything external.

## Reading a machine that does not boot

The PMD 85-3's video hardware runs from its own counters, so **sync and a
screen full of uninitialised character tiles prove nothing about the
CPU** — that is what the display looks like whenever the processor is not
running the monitor, whatever the reason. The board's own status light is
the better instrument, and `-DMHB_DIAG=ON` makes it a specific one.

The ordinary status light says whether selects are arriving:

| light | meaning |
|-------|---------|
| green / flickering | selects arriving, the machine is reading us |
| **a brief flicker, then steady red** | the machine read us a few times and **stopped** |
| steady red from the start | nothing is selecting the board at all |

That middle case is the informative one, and it is not a board fault by
itself: an 8080 fed a byte it cannot execute sensibly can land on `HLT`
(0x76), after which it fetches nothing ever again. The bus goes quiet,
the board correctly reports "nobody is asking", and the screen keeps
showing whatever was in video RAM. **A processor that stopped and a board
that stopped look identical from outside** — hence the coverage frame.

`-DMHB_DIAG=ON` replaces the status light with a four-pulse frame, one
pulse per bank, latched since power-on: long green = this bank has been
read, short red = never read. It separates the candidates in one glance:

| frame | reading |
|-------|---------|
| all four green | every bank is being served; the fault is not coverage |
| 0,1 green — 2,3 red | the X1 lead is not delivering the other pair's select |
| only one green | PR is not moving, or the pair select is stuck |
| all red | the board is never selected: socket, /CS, or power |

A frame that differs between repeats means the machine got further that
time, which is itself worth knowing. (The first frame is sampled within
microseconds of boot, before the machine has been let out of reset, so
an all-red first frame followed by a populated second one is normal.)

### The frame is history, not present state

Latched since power-on and repeating every few seconds, while the machine
does everything it will ever do in the first few milliseconds. So the
normal reading on a machine that crashes is: nothing, then a fully
populated frame that keeps repeating **after the CPU has already halted
and the selects have gone quiet**. That is the instrument working, not a
contradiction — it is reporting what happened during the burst.

Two things follow, and the second is the one that matters:

- **Coverage proves the path, not the program.** All four banks green
  says the board *can* serve every bank and the machine *did* ask for
  every bank. It says nothing about whether the bytes were the right
  ones — a processor executing garbage wanders widely, and lights banks
  for the wrong reasons.
- **A short burst is itself a measurement.** A correct mapping leaves the
  monitor running indefinitely, so the selects never stop. Activity that
  stops after a burst means the processor stopped, and an 8080 stops for
  exactly one reason it can reach by accident: it fetched a `HLT` (0x76)
  out of bytes that were never meant to be executed in that order.

### The frame locates the board, not just the fault

**All four banks green proves which pair the board is sitting in**, which
is worth more than it looks. Banks 2 and 3 can only be driven when the X1
lead reads low *while the socket's own /CS reads high* — so an all-green
frame proves the lead is on a genuinely different select net from the
socket's own. Tap X1 from a socket of the other pair and get all four
banks, and the board is provably in the pair that lead did *not* come
from.

That collapses the remaining unknowns from four to one. The socket's pair
is settled by the frame; only the PR sense is left, and it is the
difference between the two sockets of that pair — the one taking straight
A11 and the one taking it inverted. One rebuild with `MHB_PR_INVERT`
flipped covers it.
